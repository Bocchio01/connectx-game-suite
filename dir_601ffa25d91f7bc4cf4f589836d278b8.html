Detailed Description

Playing Connect 4 against a neural network on an Arduino

ConnectXNet is a sketch that runs the network trained in this repository on a microcontroller with TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers. You play from the Serial Monitor; the board answers with the move chosen by the network. A session looks like this (the numbers of the last line are illustrative: they depend on your board and settings):

=== New game ===
You are X (you move first).
. . . . . . .
. . . . . . .
. . . . . . .
. . . . . . .
. . . . . . .
. . . . . . .
1 2 3 4 5 6 7
Your move: 4
The computer plays column 4 (38 network evaluations, 311.7 ms)

Status. The sketch has not been run on real hardware yet, and the conversion scripts that produce its model (ai/python) have not been run with TensorFlow yet either. What is verified, on the PC, is everything the sketch is made of: the board code, the encoders and the search of connectx_board.h are compiled and tested against the C++ reference engine and the C++ encoders used for training (ai/tests/test_arduino_board.cpp, 400k+ checks), and the Python reader and encoders agree with the C++ ones. The TensorFlow Lite Micro calls follow the library's own hello_world example. If something does not compile or run on your board, the places to look are listed in Troubleshooting.

What you need

  • A board supported by the Chirale_TensorFlowLite library: Arduino Nano 33 BLE / Sense (the reference), Nano ESP32, Nicla, Portenta, Giga R1. 256 KB of RAM and 1 MB of flash are plenty. (The official Arduino_TensorFlowLite library was archived in 2025 and is no longer in the Library Manager; Chirale's is a maintained port of the current TensorFlow Lite Micro.)
  • Arduino IDE ≥ 2: install the board package of your board and, from Library Manager, Chirale_TensorFlowLite.
  • A model in model_data.h. The folder only contains a placeholder (compiling it stops with a message): the conversion needs TensorFlow, so the header is not generated in advance. To make it, from the pretrained network or from your own:
cd ai/python
python export_tflite.py ../models/connectx_6x7.cxnn --out-dir ../models
cp ../models/connectx_6x7_int8.h ../arduino/ConnectXNet/model_data.h

Then open ConnectXNet/ConnectXNet.ino, select the board and the port, upload, open the Serial Monitor at 115200 baud and type the number of a column (1-7). n starts a new game.

How the computer plays

The strength comes from how the network is used; the knob is SEARCH_DEPTH at the top of the sketch.

SEARCH_DEPTH The computer... Network evaluations per move Strength of this mode on the PC (C++ agent with the same logic)*
0 asks the policy head for a score per column and plays the best free one (with USE_TACTICS_SHIELD: wins/blocks first) 1 Elo 921: like an alpha-beta of depth 4 (949)
1 plays the move that leaves the position the value head likes most up to 7 Elo 890: no better than 0
2 (default) alpha-beta search of 2 plies, the value head evaluates the leaves up to 49 Elo 1090: as strong as an alpha-beta of depth 8 (1079)
3 the same, deeper up to 343 (alpha-beta prunes many) Elo 1116
4 the same, deeper up to 2401 beats 3 in 76% of the games and the depth-8 alpha-beta in 62% (60-game matches)
  • Elo of the tournament table of the AI guide (1000 = the average of that field of 15 agents, 40 games per pairing); 4 was measured in a separate small match.

Why not simply trust the policy head? Because a small network trained on a limited number of examples is far better at judging a position (the value head) than at proposing the best move; and the classic search is exactly the tool that turns a mediocre judge into a good player by looking a few moves ahead (a single ply is not enough: the gain starts at two). Wins and forced moves are always found exactly, without the network (connectx::negamax), so the network is only asked what it is good at. The sketch is therefore the same alpha-beta of the alphabeta agent with the network as evaluation function: the agent nnsearch of the C++ code.

Memory and speed

  • Flash: the model, ~1 byte per parameter (the default network has ~122 000 parameters: ~125 KB), plus the TensorFlow Lite Micro library.
  • RAM: the tensor arena (kTensorArenaSize, 24 KB by default) holds the activations; the sketch prints the bytes actually used at start-up. Increase it if AllocateTensors() fails.
  • Time: one network evaluation is ~122 000 integer multiply-accumulates. Every move prints the number of evaluations and the time it took, so you can pick the deepest SEARCH_DEPTH you find comfortable. A Cortex-M4 at 64 MHz with the CMSIS-NN kernels should do a few milliseconds per evaluation (not measured).
  • A smaller network (connectx-train supervised --hidden 64,64 ..., 18 000 parameters) is ~7 times cheaper and a little weaker: use it on boards with little memory or to search deeper.

Settings in the sketch

Setting Meaning
SEARCH_DEPTH plies of look-ahead (0 = policy head only)
USE_TACTICS_SHIELD (depth 0) always take a win / block a loss
USE_MIRROR_AVERAGING evaluate every position and its mirror image and average: a bit stronger, twice as slow
COMPUTER_STARTS who moves first
kTensorArenaSize memory for TensorFlow Lite

How it is built

  • connectx_board.h: dependency-free C++11 shared with the PC tests: the game, the three input encoders (raw, tactical, windows) and the search. CONNECTX_ROWS/COLS/CONNECT (written by the exporter into model_data.h) select the board; the default is the standard 6×7.
  • ConnectXNet.ino: TensorFlow Lite Micro set-up (a 4-operator resolver: fully connected, tanh, quantize, dequantize), int8 quantization of the input and dequantization of the outputs using the scales stored in the model, the search and the Serial interface. The two outputs of the model are told apart by their size (7 scores vs 1 value), so the order the converter gives them does not matter.

Using the older Arduino_TensorFlowLite library

Replace #include <Chirale_TensorFlowLite.h> with #include <TensorFlowLite.h> and give the interpreter an error reporter, as the library's examples do:

#include "tensorflow/lite/micro/micro_error_reporter.h"
static tflite::MicroErrorReporter micro_error_reporter;
static tflite::MicroInterpreter staticInterpreter(model, resolver, tensor_arena, kTensorArenaSize, &micro_error_reporter);

Troubleshooting

  • AllocateTensors() failed: raise kTensorArenaSize, or use a smaller network.
  • "schema version does not match": the TensorFlow version that wrote the .tflite is newer than the library's flatbuffer schema; install the latest Chirale_TensorFlowLite or export with the TensorFlow version in requirements.txt.
  • Compile error about a missing op (AddTanh, AddQuantize...): your library version names the operators differently; replace the MicroMutableOpResolver with tflite::AllOpsResolver resolver; (bigger, but it needs no list).
  • The computer plays badly or illegally: the board constants in model_data.h must match the network (the exporter writes them), and the input encoding of the model must be the one the sketch is compiled with. Evaluate the same .tflite file on the PC with ai/python/eval_model.py --tflite: if it is strong there, the problem is in the sketch; if not, in the model.
  • To test the logic without any hardware: ctest -R arduino runs the unit tests of connectx_board.h, and python play.py model.cxnn --tflite model_int8.tflite plays against the quantized model in the terminal.
