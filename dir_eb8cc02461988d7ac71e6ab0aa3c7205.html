Detailed Description

ConnectX AI

Computer players for the ConnectX Game Suite, from a random mover to a neural network trained by self-play, all written from scratch (no machine-learning library: the neural-network code is in nn/). It is meant to be read as much as to be used: every algorithm is a short, commented file.

  • a ladder of nine agents, each one a step up from the previous: random → rules → minimax → alpha-beta → exact solver → Monte Carlo tree search → neural network → neural network + search → AlphaZero;
  • a tournament tool, a training tool and a network client that lets any of them play on the ConnectX server;
  • a pretrained network and the complete road to TensorFlow Lite and the Arduino (python/, arduino/).

Try it

cmake -S . -B build && cmake --build build -j # (-DBUILD_GUI=OFF if you have no Qt and want a quiet configure)
cd build/bin
# 1. Play against the computer (three terminals; or just run ./connectx-gui --offline, see ui/gui)
./connectx-server
./connectx-cli -g "Duel<6 7 2 4>" # you
./connectx-ai -g Duel --agent alphabeta:depth=10,time=1000 # the computer
# 2. Watch the agents play each other: a round-robin tournament with Elo ratings
./connectx-arena -n 100 greedy alphabeta:depth=6 mcts:sims=2000,rollout=tactical az:model=connectx_6x7,sims=200
# 3. How good is a move picker, measured against the exact solver (positions it has never seen)
./connectx-train agent-accuracy --agent nnsearch:model=connectx_6x7,depth=3 --data models/test_positions_6x7.cxds

connectx-ai --list-agents describes every agent and its parameters. An agent is written type:key=value,...: random, greedy, minimax:depth=5, alphabeta:depth=10,time=1000, solver:time=3000, mcts:sims=5000,rollout=tactical, nn:model=..., nnsearch:model=...,depth=3, az:model=...,sims=400. Models are looked up in models/ next to the executables, so connectx_6x7 is a complete model name.

The ladder of agents

# Agent The idea Where
0 random a legal move at random: the baseline simple_agents.cpp
1 greedy one move of look-ahead: win now, else block the opponent's win, else avoid moves that hand him a win, else the centre simple_agents.cpp
2 minimax explore every sequence of moves to a fixed depth and score the leaves with a hand-written evaluation (open lines) minimax_agent.cpp
3 alphabeta the same value as minimax, with pruning (skip branches that cannot matter), move ordering, exact tactics; with time= also iterative deepening and a transposition table alphabeta_agent.cpp
4 solver perfect play: exhaustive alpha-beta made fast by bitboards, a transposition table and null-window search. Time-limited, then falls back on 3 solver.cpp
5 mcts Monte Carlo tree search: no evaluation function; play thousands of random games and keep statistics (UCT) mcts_agent.cpp
6 nn the policy head of the network alone: one forward pass, no search neural_agent.cpp
7 nnsearch alpha-beta (level 3) whose evaluation function is the network's value head neural_search_agent.cpp
8 az AlphaZero: Monte Carlo tree search (level 5) guided by both heads of the network (PUCT) alphazero.cpp

Everything stands on a fast game representation, the bitboard Position (a whole 6×7 board in two 64-bit words, win detection in a dozen instructions), which is checked against the reference engine of core/ by millions of random positions in the unit tests. Boards up to cols × (rows + 1) ≤ 64 bits (6×7, 7×7, 7×8, 5×9, 4×12...) with any connect length and two players are supported.

How strong are the agents?

Two independent measurements, both made with tools of this repository (connectx-arena and connectx-train agent-accuracy).

1. Playing against each other. A round-robin tournament on the standard 6×7 board: every agent plays every other agent 40 games (in pairs that share a random two-move opening, with the colours swapped), i.e. 560 games per agent, 4 minutes on a laptop. The Elo rating only tells how an agent does against this field (1000 = the average of the field); "ms/move" is the average thinking time.

What the table shows:

  • The ladder works: random 295 < greedy 713 < the depth-4 searches ~950 < alpha-beta depth 8 1079 < ... < az 1342.
  • Pruning is free speed: minimax and alphabeta of depth 4 play the same (947 and 949: they compute the same values), but alpha-beta needs a fifth of the time. Looking four moves further (depth 8, 6 ms per move) is worth 130 Elo.
  • The simulation policy matters more than the number of simulations: MCTS with random rollouts and 1000 simulations (932) is weaker than a depth-4 search; with the "tactical" rollout (take a win, avoid a move that loses at once) the same 1000 simulations reach 1129.
  • The network alone plays like a depth-4 search (nn, 921) with one forward pass of 0.02 ms, five times faster. As the evaluation function of an alpha-beta search (nnsearch) it is much better: with 2 plies (1090) it equals the classic depth-8 search in a tenth of the time. (With a single ply the value head is no improvement on the policy head; the gain appears from two plies on.)
  • AlphaZero is the strongest: az with 400 simulations (8 ms per move) beats every other agent, including MCTS with 25 times more simulations (70% of the points) and alpha-beta searching 14 plies for up to 200 ms (76%).
  • Differences of a few tens of Elo (for example ranks 3 and 4) are within the noise of 40 games per pairing.

The exact solver (level 4) is not in the table: it plays perfectly, but only when it has time to finish (about 0.1 s with 12 stones on the board, half a second to 6 seconds with 6-8 stones, 16 minutes for the empty board), and when the time limit expires it falls back on alpha-beta, so in a timed tournament it would just be an alpha-beta of unknown depth. Its job is to be the referee of the second measurement.

2. Choosing the right move where the answer is known. 2,396 positions that the networks never saw in training ([models/test_positions_6x7.cxds](models)), labelled by the exact solver. In a winning position (2,000 of them) a move is right if the player still wins with perfect play afterwards; in a drawn position (396) if the draw is kept. A random mover is not at zero: in many positions several moves are good, or the game is won whatever you do.

How the pieces work

The exact solver (level 4)

Connect-4 is a solved game: with perfect play the first player wins by starting in the centre column. The Solver computes the game-theoretic value of any position with negamax and alpha-beta, and the ingredients that make it fast are the interesting part: (1) the bitboard position, where playing a move and testing for a win are a few bit operations; (2) moves that lose at once are never searched, and a forced move is played without searching; (3) moves that create the most threats are tried first; (4) a transposition table remembers positions already analysed; (5) instead of one big search, a sequence of cheap null-window searches ("is the score above `m`?") narrows the score down by bisection. It is verified against an independent exhaustive search on small boards and against a full-depth search of real positions, including the speed of the win. Solving a position with 12 stones takes ~0.1 s on average and one with 8 stones a few seconds; the empty board took 16 minutes (1.6 billion positions examined, on a laptop, with a 16-million-entry table) and confirmed that the first player wins.

It also acts as the teacher of the neural networks: positions labelled by the solver are the training data.

Alpha-beta and its relatives (levels 2-3)

Minimax assumes both players choose the best move for themselves; alpha-beta computes exactly the same value but stops exploring a branch as soon as it is provably irrelevant: with good move ordering it needs about the square root of the nodes. The test suite checks that alphabeta and minimax return identical values on thousands of positions while alphabeta visits several times fewer nodes. The hand-written evaluation counts, for every line of four cells, how many stones one player has in it when the other has none.

Monte Carlo tree search (level 5)

No knowledge of the game but its rules. Four steps repeated thousands of times: select a path down the tree following the UCT rule (average result + an exploration bonus for rarely tried moves), expand a leaf, simulate a game to the end with a cheap policy, back-propagate the result. The simulation policy matters more than anything else: rollout=tactical (take a win, avoid a move that loses at once) is dramatically stronger than random rollouts.

Neural networks (levels 6-8)

A two-headed network (nn/) reads the board and produces a policy (a score for each column) and a value (who is winning). The input encoding (encoding.hpp) is a design decision of its own: with raw the network only sees two planes of stones and has to discover what a "three in a row" is; with tactical (default) it is also shown the cells where either player would complete four and the playable cells, so tactics become visible and the network can spend its capacity on strategy. On the same data this is worth ~3 points of accuracy.

What the experiments taught, and what the numbers above show:

  • the policy head alone is a modest player (about as strong as a depth-4 search): even when it picks a result-preserving move ~89% of the time, the 11% of mistakes accumulate over a game;
  • the value head is much more useful, and the classical search is the right tool to exploit it: nnsearch with the same network is far stronger than nn, and a depth of 2 is already on par with a classic alpha-beta of depth 8 (which takes ten times longer to think);
  • with a tree search driven by both heads (az) the network becomes a very strong player.

AlphaZero (level 8)

AlphaZeroSearch is Monte Carlo tree search in which the network replaces both weak points of the plain version: a new leaf is not played out but evaluated by the value head, and the policy head gives every move a prior probability that steers the selection (the PUCT rule Q + c·P·√N / (1 + n)). The visit counts at the root are a better policy than the one the network started from.

Training by self-play (selfplay.cpp) closes the loop: the network plays games against itself with this search (exploring with Dirichlet noise at the root and by sampling the early moves), every position is stored with the visit counts as the policy target and the final result as the value target, and the network is trained on a replay buffer of recent games. A better network gives a better search, which gives better targets. Our network starts from the supervised one (below) and keeps a share of solver-labelled positions in every batch ("teacher" data), which anchors the value head to the truth in the early iterations.

Training your own network

cd build/bin
# 1. Labelled positions: games of a noisy alpha-beta player; the exact solver labels the positions
# (win/draw/loss of every move). ~20 positions/s on 4 cores: this is the expensive step (50 minutes).
./connectx-train gen-data --out data.cxds --count 60000 --threads 4
# 2. Supervised learning: the network imitates the solver (a few minutes)
./connectx-train supervised --data data.cxds --out supervised.cxnn --hidden 256,256 --encoding tactical --epochs 45
# 3. Self-play reinforcement learning, starting from the supervised network and with teacher data mixed in
./connectx-train selfplay --out-dir run --init supervised.cxnn --teacher data.cxds --teacher-fraction 0.3 \
--iterations 120 --games 300 --sims 200 --steps 400 --buffer 200000
# 4. Measure it
./connectx-arena -n 100 greedy alphabeta:depth=6 nnsearch:model=run/latest.cxnn,depth=2 az:model=run/latest.cxnn,sims=100
./connectx-train agent-accuracy --agent nn:model=run/latest.cxnn --data test.cxds
# 5. TensorFlow, TensorFlow Lite, Arduino: see python/README.md and arduino/README.md

connectx-train --help lists every command and option: gen-data, supervised, selfplay, inspect, dataset-info, merge-data, eval, agent-accuracy, export-vectors. The pretrained model of this repository was made with these commands (the data in four runs of 15,000 positions with different seeds, joined by merge-data, which drops the duplicates): about an hour and three quarters on a 4-core laptop CPU, no GPU, of which 50 minutes of data generation, 3 of supervised learning and 45 of self-play. Models and datasets are documented binary files (nn/README.md, python/cxnn.py).

What small experiments said

Before the long runs, each design question was settled with a short supervised experiment: the same 30,000 labelled positions (27,000 to train on, 3,000 held out), 40 epochs, 1-3 minutes each. The last column is the share of held-out positions where the network's best move preserves the game-theoretic result.

Showing the network the tactics is worth about as much as doubling its width, and doubling the data barely moved the needle: at this scale the limit is what an MLP can represent, not how many positions it has seen.

Things to try

The code is small enough to be changed, and the tools tell you what the change did:

  1. Break the pruning. alphabeta_agent.cpp tries the columns from the centre outwards (position.hpp). Try them left to right and compare the nodes per move (connectx-ai -v) and the thinking time of alphabeta:depth=8: alpha-beta is only as good as its move ordering.
  2. Write an agent. Derive from Agent (agent.hpp: name(), chooseMove(), seed()) and add it to createAgent() and availableAgents() in factory.cpp: it is then available to connectx-arena, connectx-ai and agent-accuracy. Good first candidates: a 1-ply agent that maximises the evaluation function of level 2, or MCTS with a rollout of your own.
  3. Tune the search. Pit az:model=connectx_6x7,sims=200,c=0.5 against c=1.5 and c=4 in the arena, or do the same with the exploration constant c of mcts: the best values are not obvious.
  4. Change what the network sees or learns. Train with --encoding raw or --hidden 32,32, or with --target best / --target soft instead of outcome (connectx-train supervised --help), and compare with agent-accuracy: the table of experiments above was made exactly like this.
  5. Self-play without a teacher. --teacher-fraction 0 removes the solver-labelled positions from the batches and leaving out --init starts from a random network: how many iterations does it take to beat greedy?
  6. Another board. Add --rows 5 --cols 6 to connectx-arena and to gen-data / selfplay (the bot plays whatever board its game has): on small boards the exact solver finishes quickly, so you can compare a network trained for that board with perfect play (solver).
  7. Squeeze it onto the Arduino. Train with --hidden 64,64 (7 times cheaper) and see how much strength a deeper SEARCH_DEPTH buys back (arduino/).

Limits

  • Two-player games, on boards that fit the bitboard (cols × (rows + 1) ≤ 64); the AI client refuses other games with an explanation. The pretrained network is for the standard 6×7 board with connect 4; the search agents work on any supported board and connectx-train can train a network for any of them.
  • The networks are multilayer perceptrons: there are no convolutions (they would be ~100 times slower to train on a CPU).
  • Positions with fewer than 8 stones cannot be labelled by the solver in reasonable time: the supervised data covers the middle game and the endgame, the opening is learned by self-play.
